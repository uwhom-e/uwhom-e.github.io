A few notes behind the choices:
- **Servo control board:** two per robot, so a spare is on hand if one burns out during testing
- **Wheel motors:** 700 CPR encoders give about 2,797 counts per output revolution with full quadrature, or roughly 0.09 mm of travel per count on an 80 mm wheel. Buying through a distributor avoids the ~$130 and duties of ordering direct
- **Motor driver:** each board drives two motors, with the Cytron Shield-MDD10 as the alternative
- **LiDAR:** at 2 m, neighbouring points are about 2 cm apart, which is enough to pick out a teammate's base
- **ToF sensors:** eight multizone sensors cover both horizontal and vertical field of view for close-range object detection around the robot
- **IMU:** the same BNO055 we have used before, so the driver code carries over
- **Wrist camera:** still open, and not yet in the total

**Sponsorship package.** Helped put together the project pitch the team is taking to potential sponsors. It introduces HOM-E, the gap left by fixed-torso two-armed robots, and our problem statement (locate, coordinate, react), and closes with the per-robot cost breakdown above so a sponsor can see exactly what their contribution covers. The arm servos are the first target, which is why the BOM also tracks the total without them.

**Electrical system.** Laid out the hardware architecture for one robot, covering both how power is distributed and how every component talks to the compute.

![Hardware architecture diagram for one robot, showing power distribution on the left and compute, sensors and actuators on the right](./2026-11-02-electrical-system.png)

*Power.* A 12 V 3S battery feeds everything through a 30 A main fuse and an ideal diode module (rated 4–60 V / 25 A), so a battery connected backwards can't damage anything. Just after the diode, a 2 mΩ shunt with an INA226 current monitor tracks the robot's total power draw and reports it to the ESP32 over I2C. From there the supply splits:
- **Logic:** a 5 A fuse into a 5 V buck that powers the Raspberry Pi 5 and the ESP32-S3, with a second 5 V buck supplying the sensors
- **Actuators:** a 30 A relay, switched by an ESP32 GPIO through a MOSFET with a physical E-stop in the coil circuit, feeds three 10 A fused branches: one for the servo control board and one for each motor driver board

Putting the actuators behind the relay means either the E-stop or the ESP32 can cut power to every motor and servo at once, while the compute stays powered.

*Signals.* The two controllers split the work by speed:
- **Raspberry Pi 5 (high level):** the servo control board and RGB-D camera over USB 3.0, the SLAMTEC LiDAR over UART, and the eight ToF sensors on I2C, with their LPn lines on GPIO to give each one its own address
- **ESP32-S3 (low level):** connected to the Pi over USB-C, it drives both motor driver boards with PWM and direction signals and reads the IMU over I2C

#### Karthigan

##### Software Architecture & DAG Execution

**Expanded the software architecture proposal.** Built on the preliminary research with a proposal connecting a shared task planner and execution layer to distributed robot coordination and local feedback control. Defined how reusable skills should describe participants, prerequisites, completion conditions and conditions that must remain true during execution, such as maintaining a grasp while carrying. Also investigated grasp/contact selection, load sharing, reachability, collision checks and uncertainty, and outlined interfaces and recovery paths for integrating these components.

**Built an executable co-carry DAG prototype.** Added a task-graph runner on top of the existing two-robot MuJoCo simulator. A strict JSON schema describes the actions, assigned robots, parameters, dependencies and timeouts. The executor starts actions when their predecessors have succeeded and their robots are available, allowing independent actions to run concurrently while joint actions reserve both robots.

Co-carry is composed from six separately executable primitives: **approach, grasp, lift, transport, lower and release**. Completion is checked against simulated motion, contact and support rather than advancing through a single fixed timeline. Holding state persists between nodes, and detected failures or timeouts stop the simulation episode and block pending actions.

**Made grasp locations explicit.** Approach and grasp both require a named `grasp_site`: `end_a` or `end_b`. These sites are attached to the payload's coordinate frame, so their positions and orientations follow the object. The example plans assign r0 to `end_a` and r1 to `end_b`; a grasp requesting a different site from its completed approach is rejected. The selected site and grasp orientation are retained through the subsequent phases and withdrawal.

**Tested different graph structures in the simulator.** All three co-carry plans completed the lift, transport, placement and release task using simulated finger contact. The dependency changes alter execution without changing the primitive implementations:

| DAG structure | Execution pattern | Simulated completion time |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| Parallel | Each robot grasps as soon as its own approach finishes | 18.728 s |
| Staged | Both approaches finish before either grasp starts | 18.728 s |
| Serial | r0 grasps before r1 begins its approach | 23.008 s |

The **35 automated tests** cover schema validation, dependency scheduling, robot ownership, failure handling and grasp-site behavior, including transformed objects, mismatched sites and stale approach positions. Each run also produces node-level events, state observations and a result report for comparing DAG experiments.

The current prototype uses MuJoCo ground-truth feedback and scripted coordinated motion. The proposed perception, grasp-planning and cooperative-control components remain integration work for subsequent iterations.
